*(Contains spoilers.)*

I don't think Elizabeth Holmes ever intended to deceive anyone. Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim's documentary (two hours and fifty-four minutes, an A24 release) follows Elizabeth Holmes through the thirty-four days before she reported to prison in 2023. Then it follows her into prison. The film reconstructs it with Amanda Seyfried (who played Holmes in *The Dropout*) standing in for Holmes in the conversations the cameras couldn’t capture. The crew lived in the rented beach house in Del Mar with Holmes, her husband Billy Evans, and their two small children. The whole thing circles one question: did Elizabeth Holmes deceive investors, and did she intend to?

The film never answers it. That is the point. My answer, after sitting with it: I don't think she intended to deceive anyone.

## Fielder wins by pressing, then waiting

Nathan Fielder is uncannily good at interviewing. It's his personality. I don't know if he's on the autism spectrum (he's never said he is), but he's a master at holding his frame, asking questions, and waiting.

He translates his comedic style into investigative journalism. He is better than anyone I have ever watched at:

1. pressing people when they say something, and
2. waiting.

He holds the awkwardness in a way even good television journalists can't. It gets socially awkward, but he has cultivated a persona that allows people to expect it. That lets people open up and say things a normal television interview would not get.

And then there is the access itself. The whole idea is living in somebody's house and talking to them with their kids. I've never seen investigative journalism go that deep into somebody's life.

## Two analogies explain how Holmes thinks

Nathan's brain is wired for analogies. He uses two in the documentary, and both land.

The first is the Abbott and Costello sketch "Who's on First?" He shows it to Holmes and Billy, and she laughs. Then she insists she never understood that the first baseman's name was "Who," which is the crux of the joke. She doesn't understand the skit. And it works as a probe of something deeper: her relationship with language. Theranos's whole pitch turned on the word "can" (the device *can* run hundreds of tests). The film keeps pressing on that word. It lives in the gap between what a device can do in principle and what it can do right now.

The second is David Blaine. Fielder tells Holmes she reminds him of Blaine's ice-pick trick, where the magician appears to drive an ice pick through his hand. Except the trick is that there is no trick: Blaine really does it, driving the pick through a channel of scar tissue he has built up in his palm over years of doing it. It's an apt analogy that cuts two ways. We keep looking for the hidden mechanism, the secret, and there isn't one. And at the same time, the person is really doing the thing, really enduring it, not faking it. Fielder says at one point that talking to Holmes felt like watching a magician: she seemed authentic, and he didn't believe her, at the same time.

## Holmes was not lying so much as dangerously optimistic

Is Elizabeth Holmes lying? This turns on intent and culpability in law. The American legal system broadly holds that you have to intend to do something wrong. In Latin it's mens rea, a guilty mind. That test asks what she meant, not what she did. The jury found she defrauded investors of nearly $145 million, and the whole trial turned on whether she meant to.

She never intended to deceive anyone.

She has something a lot of CEOs have: radical techno-optimism, combined with a poor ability to understand other people. She reads to me as someone who is probably on the spectrum (I don't know that, and there is no diagnosis on record), and that may make it difficult for her to contemplate the impact of her words on others.

She's smart, but she doesn't understand "Who's on First?"

She seems to have believed her testing kit was going to run all kinds of tests based on biochemistry that was possible. She believed she was building a platform that would do it. She's so optimistic, so techno-optimistic, that she assumed it would happen.

This level of optimism doesn't belong in investor pitches. Elon Musk talks about this. His line is: "I'd rather be optimistic and wrong than pessimistic and right."

Optimism is great for generating ideas and progress, but it's not good for evaluating what a company is worth. The dreamer was also the one telling investors what the company was worth. Theranos gave investors a forecast of $140 million in revenue for 2014, after earning none in 2012 or 2013. The wrong person was pitching this company, and nothing checked her optimism. It makes me wonder, *why wasn’t someone checking the numbers*.

Elizabeth Holmes is not cut out to sell things to people. She should be barred from ever selling anything to anyone again, because she's mentally incapable of staying in the present moment and describing the technical details of her product or service. (The SEC did bar her from serving as an officer or director of a public company for ten years, in its 2018 settlement. That's not a ban on selling anything, but the same instinct.)

## A dreamer attracts the men who exploit dreamers

This is the second part of the problem: what you put out into the world is what you attract. Elizabeth seems to attract men who take advantage of smart people with great ideas. Those people ignore the day-to-day money. In the film, Billy negotiates a cut of the profits while Elizabeth tells Nathan she has no economic interest in it. That's where things broke.

## Everyone in the film has good and bad intentions at once

We all have a natural urge to find a villain and a hero in any story. There is no hero and no villain here. That is what makes the story unsettling and fascinating. The documentary shows that everyone had good and bad intentions at once.

Billy, Elizabeth Holmes's husband, seems to love his kids, and the love between him and Elizabeth is genuine and strong. At the same time, it's this terrible mess that's wreaking havoc on others and is probably going to defraud more people. Nathan spots this: the mess can be real and the love can still be genuine. Elizabeth's trust in Billy is genuine, and Billy's desire to make things happen for Elizabeth and to drive the company forward is genuine. I don't see much intent to deceive. It's more like gross negligence, driven not by intention but by personality.

Many people said they felt sorry for Elizabeth Holmes, but I don't.

Some people aren't suited to selling. When those people are smart, they often slip through the cracks, succeed, and screw over investors. That's where I sit.

## The film leaves the verdict to the viewer

The movie hints at a theory but leaves the verdict to the viewer, which comes from Nathan's habit of pausing and letting his interviewees talk. That is the film's real achievement. It refuses the simple shape, the villain and the hero. It sits in the discomfort of a woman who may have done enormous damage without ever intending to. It also sits in a system that had no way to stop her because it rewards exactly the optimism that made her dangerous.

Go see it. It is the most unsettling and the most fascinating film I have seen about Silicon Valley, and about what happens when the person selling the future cannot see the present.
